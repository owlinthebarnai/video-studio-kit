# The copywriter

Use this to write the story before any code. The words decide whether people stay, and every line has to give them a reason to see the next style.

## The shape

Ten lines is plenty. Most good ones follow this:

1. **Someone wants something.** First line, so the viewer knows who to root for.
2. **They try.** They build, leave, start.
3. **It takes off.** The loud moment.
4. **It's working.** Short, fast, a line or two.
5. **It goes wrong.** Stop the music here if there is music.
6. **They do something about it.** The simpler and funnier, the better.
7. **It pays off.** They get what they wanted.
8. **The twist.** One thing the viewer didn't see coming. It can change what the whole story meant.

For fewer beats, keep lines 1, 5, 6 and 7. Fewer styles than beats is fine: repeat them, and try ending in the style you started in.

## How to write a line

- One line for each beat, 3 to 9 words. Read it out loud. If you run out of breath or it sounds written, rewrite it.
- Something changes in every line. If the story would work without it, cut it.
- Write what happens, using things you can draw: "the engine died", not "they faced a setback".
- Use small words. A 10-year-old should get every line on the first listen.
- Don't describe the picture. The voice says the part the picture can't.
- Don't name the styles in the story. The look does that.

## Matching lines to styles

| The moment | Styles that suit it |
|---|---|
| The quiet start | Pixel art, paper cutout |
| Setting a goal | PS2 style, because a mission on screen says it for you |
| The loud moment | Comic art, retro anime |
| Building or growing | Lego style, claymation |
| Things go wrong | Paper cutout, because the stop-motion feels fragile |
| The turn, the big effort | Retro anime, with one impact frame |
| Obstacles, speed | Low poly 3D, PS2 style |
| The win, a poster moment | Risograph |
| The reveal | Miniature diorama |

## For an app or a SaaS

Put the product at the turn, where the story needs it most. It's the thing that fixes the problem. Keep it to one clear shot, and don't explain features. End on what the person got, then the call to action.

An example, for a made-up app that finds lost keys:

- Mia was late again.
- Her keys could be anywhere.
- She tore the whole house apart.
- The couch. The freezer. The dog.
- Nothing.
- Then she opened Keyfind.
- One tap, and something started to beep.
- They were in her hand the whole time.
- Keyfind. Link in bio.

That's 9 beats. The app arrives on the sixth, and the laugh on the eighth is what people send to a friend. The last line is the only one that sells.

## Before you show it

- Read the whole thing out loud with a timer. Aim for 15 to 25 seconds.
- Could a stranger retell the story from the words alone?
- Is there a line that only sounds good? Cut it.
- Take out anything that sounds like a robot wrote it: "not just X but Y", "in a world where", "little did they know", "it's more than a", em dashes, and lists of three when two will do.
